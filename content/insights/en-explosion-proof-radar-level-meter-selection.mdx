# How to Select an Explosion-Proof Radar Level Meter: A Practical Guide for Hazardous-Area Tanks

Selecting an explosion-proof radar level meter for a chemical tank, solvent tank, fuel tank, or process vessel is not a matter of finding the word *explosion-proof* in a catalogue. Radar can measure without contacting the medium, but the instrument and installation may still be located where flammable gas, vapour, or combustible dust is present. A defensible selection connects the hazardous-area basis, equipment documentation, process conditions, mechanical installation, and electrical loop.

This article is a project-selection guide, not a substitute for hazardous-area classification, regulatory requirements, approval by competent engineers, or the manufacturer’s current documentation. The applicable local rules, site drawings, project specification, and valid product documents remain the controlling sources.

## Start With the Hazardous-Area Basis

The first question is not “Which radar model is explosion-proof?” It is “What is the defined condition at this exact instrument location?” The project team should obtain the current hazardous-area drawing and understand the release source, material involved, ventilation assumptions, extent of the classified area, and the relevant zone or division designation. Dust hazards must be evaluated in their own right; a statement about a flammable-vapour installation cannot automatically be reused for a combustible-dust location.

Two vessels that appear similar may have very different conditions at the nozzle where the instrument will be installed. One could be in an open, well-ventilated area; another could sit near a vent, loading connection, enclosed structure, or dust-generating process. A generic purchase specification is not a replacement for this site-specific information.

| Information to obtain | Questions to resolve | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Area documentation | What classification applies at the sensor and junction-box locations? | It is the basis for checking permitted equipment and installation conditions. |
| Material and process data | Is there flammable vapour, gas, mist, or combustible dust? What are the operating states? | Determines what must be considered in the hazardous-area and process review. |
| Environmental exposure | Is the installation exposed to rain, washdown, salt, condensation, temperature cycling, or corrosion? | Influences enclosure, sealing, materials, and accessories. |
| Vessel geometry | What nozzle, flange, roof, internal structures, and access constraints exist? | Affects beam clearance, false echoes, process connection, and maintainability. |
| Electrical architecture | What power, signal, isolation, grounding, and control-system arrangements are required? | Defines the complete loop and field-installation requirements. |

When this information is incomplete, it is safer to resolve it before procurement than to assume that a broadly described “hazardous-area model” will cover the final application.

## Check Certification as a Complete System Question

A review should compare the exact ordered model, nameplate data, certificate or declaration, and project requirement. Do not stop at a certificate number. The documentation may define conditions related to ambient temperature, process temperature, installation orientation, cable entries, permitted accessories, or other limitations. Those conditions are part of the decision.

The sensor is also rarely the only item in the field. A display, remote electronics unit, junction box, cable gland, connector, isolator, power supply, or antenna accessory may have its own applicability and installation conditions. A claim made for one item does not automatically extend to a completed loop that combines components from several suppliers.

Where an intrinsically safe arrangement is required, competent project personnel should evaluate the complete loop, including the field device, associated apparatus or isolator, wiring, earthing approach, and the relevant entity or loop parameters. Other protection concepts also have installation rules that must be followed exactly. Opening a housing, changing a connector, substituting a cable gland, or adding an unapproved accessory can alter conditions assumed by the original approval.

### Do Not Confuse Explosion Protection With Other Protections

Explosion protection, ingress protection, corrosion resistance, and electromagnetic compatibility are separate topics. Hazardous-area documentation does not by itself prove that cable entries resist corrosion, a junction box remains dry, or an outdoor station has an adequate surge and grounding design. Review the process connection, sealing, exposed materials, enclosure, and support hardware for the actual site; apply the project requirements for bonding, shielding, grounding, and surge protection.

## Match the Radar to the Process, Not Just the Tank Height

Radar level measurement generally relies on a transmitted electromagnetic signal and a returned reflection from the medium surface. Performance depends on the surface and installation conditions. Low-dielectric media, thick foam, vapour, condensation, turbulent filling, agitation, and internal structures can all influence echo identification and signal quality.

A useful data sheet describes more than “liquid.” Include the medium name, process temperature and pressure, expected vapour, foam behaviour, agitation, filling stream, normal and abnormal level range, and any cleaning or coating mechanism. For volatile or corrosive service, verify material compatibility for the process connection, seals, antenna region, and any component exposed to vapour or condensate. High-temperature, high-pressure, or vacuum service requires particular attention to the process connection and sealing arrangement specified for the actual device.

Non-contact measurement reduces direct immersion of the sensing face in the liquid. It does not eliminate the need to check materials, seals, process pressure boundaries, or corrosive atmospheric exposure around the nozzle.

## Installation Geometry Is a Measurement Requirement

The preferred mounting point gives the radar a clear view of the expected liquid surface. Avoid locations where a filling stream, agitator, heating coil, ladder, roof support, or nearby wall produces persistent reflections or disruptive surface movement. Long nozzles, narrow connections, and unsuitable angles can restrict the beam path or add multipath reflections. On vessels with strong surface motion, avoid the most severe impact or vortex zone where practical.

The mechanical installation must also remain serviceable. Keep access for inspection, allow for required clearances, and implement the process connection and fastening method in accordance with the approved design. Route cables so that water does not track toward enclosures; seal unused entries as instructed; and apply wiring, shielding, and grounding to the project drawings.

Commissioning records should be traceable. They may include the installed model, nozzle or mounting height, configured reference distances, photographs of the installation, connection checks, and records of identified fixed echoes or abnormal conditions. These records make later fault investigation and maintenance more efficient.

## A Practical Selection Workflow

### 1. Assemble the design inputs

Collect the current hazardous-area drawing, process data sheet, vessel drawing, utility and control-interface requirements, and applicable project standards. Resolve conflicts before issuing a purchase order.

### 2. Define the measurement duty

Specify whether the instrument supports continuous indication, inventory measurement, high/low alarm functions, operational control, or a protective function. Define the required measuring span, normal operating range, update needs, and communications or analogue interface without implying capabilities that are not documented for the selected device.

### 3. Review documented applicability

Compare the exact instrument configuration and each relevant accessory against the project’s hazardous-area, process, and environmental requirements. Verify the current documents and their installation conditions, rather than relying on an old catalogue image or a generic family description.

### 4. Verify process connection and materials

Confirm nozzle type, flange or thread, pressure and temperature conditions, seal selection, antenna arrangement, and exposed materials. Include corrosion, fouling, and maintenance access in the review.

### 5. Review mounting and loop design

Check beam clearance, internal obstructions, cable path, junction boxes, isolation or associated apparatus where applicable, earthing, and surge measures. Assign responsibility for the final hazardous-area installation review.

### 6. Define acceptance evidence

Build a handover package that records installed nameplates, associated documentation, approved accessories, cable entries, enclosure closure, installation photographs, wiring checks, and functional tests. A traceable record is more useful than an unsupported assertion that the installation is “explosion-proof.”

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can an explosion-proof radar level meter be used in every hazardous location?

No. Suitability depends on the site’s classification, the relevant gas/vapour or dust conditions, the exact equipment documentation, and the way it is installed. Confirm these together through the project’s established engineering process.

### Does non-contact radar eliminate material-compatibility concerns?

No. The radar may measure the surface from above, but the process connection, seals, antenna area, exposed hardware, and surrounding atmosphere can still be affected by vapour, condensate, corrosion, pressure, and temperature.

### May installers replace a cable gland or junction box in the field?

Treat this as a controlled engineering change, not a casual substitution. A replacement can affect sealing, the electrical loop, and the conditions under which the equipment documentation applies. Check the approved accessory list, instructions, and site procedure first.

### What should be checked during handover?

At minimum, verify the installed model and nameplate, relevant documents, process connection, cable entries, enclosure closure, wiring and grounding arrangements, mounting geometry, and successful functional checks. The exact inspection scope should follow the project and regulatory requirements.

## Conclusion

A reliable explosion-proof radar level measurement project begins with the hazardous-area and process information, then carries those requirements through documentation review, materials, installation geometry, and electrical-loop design. Treating the instrument, accessories, cables, mounting, and records as one engineering system produces a more traceable and maintainable result than selecting by label alone.
